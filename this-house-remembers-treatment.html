This House Remembers

A son returns home with the deed to a new house for his ageing father, only to discover that the old compound is holding the confession neither man has spoken for fifteen years.

Short filmNigeriaIjoko, Ogun State15 minutes · 2.39:1
Short Synopsis

Tunde believes he has built his father a safer future. Across one day, a bottle filled with water, pencil marks on a doorframe, a folded wrapper, a sealed letter, and a red hibiscus reveal why Baba Ayo cannot leave the house where love, guilt, and memory took root.

Overview

A restrained family drama about the difference between rescuing someone and truly seeing them, told across one compound and one day that ends where it began: at the hibiscus.

Act One — The Gift

Morning begins with ritual. Baba Ayo waters the red hibiscus outside the old family house in Ijoko, moving with the exactness of a man who has repeated the same acts for years. Tunde arrives carrying wonderful news: after six years of quiet payments, he has bought his father a furnished house near his own family, with water, electricity, security, and a clinic close by. He expects relief. Baba Ayo receives the deed without celebration and goes inside to fry his son an egg the way Tunde’s mother always insisted it should be done.

At breakfast, affection survives through habit because neither man knows how to speak it directly. Baba Ayo pours water from an old brown beer bottle and sets Tunde’s gift bottle aside unopened. The gesture appears stubborn, almost comic, but the house begins to complicate Tunde’s certainty. His father is not simply refusing comfort. He is obeying rules whose meaning has been buried.

Act Two — What the Rooms Keep

Tunde follows his father through the compound, trying to turn practical arguments into agreement. Each room answers differently. At the dining table lives his mother’s rule about talking while they eat. In the parlour, grandchildren smile from a phone screen while the unsigned deed waits beside them. In the corridor, pencil marks climb a doorframe: Tunde and his brother Dele measured year after year, two sons preserved in graphite long after the family stopped being able to stand together.

In the bedroom, the objects become more intimate. Baba Ayo keeps his wife’s indigo-and-gold wrapper folded with care. A garri tin recalls the money once hidden inside it and the land secretly sold to pay a boy’s school fees. Tunde begins to understand that the sacrifices he thought belonged to him were part of a larger history. The new house he has built is generous, but it is also an answer to a question he never asked.

Act Three — The Confession

At dusk, father and son return to the yard. The watering can tips. Baba Ayo finally speaks about the night his wife died alone and about the failure he has converted into fifteen years of private punishment. Dele, who found her, survives in the house as pencil marks, a sealed letter, and a name neither man can finish. Baba Ayo has remained not because the building is sound, but because leaving feels like abandoning the last place where his guilt can still serve as devotion.

Tunde does not force the deed into his father’s hand. Baba Ayo cuts a piece from the hibiscus for his granddaughter: memory allowed to travel without being uprooted. The sealed letter is put back, not destroyed and not yet opened. At dawn, the watering begins again. Nothing has been neatly repaired, but the ritual has changed. What looked like refusal is now understood as grief, and the house no longer keeps the truth alone.
